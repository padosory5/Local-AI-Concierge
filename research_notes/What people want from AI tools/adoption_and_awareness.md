# Adoption, retention, abandonment and awareness of AI tools (and why almost nobody has heard of local AI)

Notes compiled 2026-11-02. Data from 2025 and 2026 unless marked **[older]**. **[secondary]** means I could not open the primary source and am relying on press or blog coverage. **[unverified]** means the figure circulates but I found no primary source. Quotes are kept under 15 words; everything else is paraphrase. Surveys disagree a lot, mostly because of question wording, panel type and age range; the conflicts are listed, not smoothed over.

## 1. What share of people use AI tools, by country and age, how often, and how has this changed?

### Takeaway
Roughly half of adults in rich countries now use a chatbot at least sometimes, and about a quarter use one daily. Daily use is the most consistent number across surveys (24 to 28%). Age is the biggest divider everywhere: under-30s use it at three to ten times the rate of over-65s. South Korea moved from below average to well above average in about a year (roughly 31 to 45% of the population by Korean government surveys, 40.6% of working-age people by Microsoft's estimate). Usage among Koreans in their 60s and 70s is still very low (6.3% and 0.7% in one large panel).

### Cited Findings

**United States**
- Pew (American Trends Panel, probability-based, n=5,119 adults, fielded Feb 17 to 23, 2026): 49% of US adults use AI chatbots, up from 33% in 2024. The 2024 question was asked only of people who had heard of chatbots, so the trend is not exactly like-for-like. — [Pew, Americans and AI 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/)
- Pew, same survey, frequency: 24% use chatbots daily (8% about once a day, 12% several times a day, 4% almost constantly), 25% several times a week or less, 51% do not use them. — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/)
- Pew by age, any chatbot use (2026 vs 2024): 18 to 29: 66% (55%); 30 to 49: 61% (41%); 50 to 64: 42% (25%); 65+: 23% (12%). Daily use: 18 to 29: 31%; 30 to 49: 34%; 50 to 64: 19%; 65+: 7%. The 30 to 49 group, not the youngest, has the highest daily use. — [Pew, use by age](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/how-opinions-and-use-of-ai-differ-by-age/)
- Pew, which chatbots (ever use, 2026): ChatGPT 44%, Gemini 24%, Copilot 17%, Meta AI 14%, Grok 8%, Claude 6%, Character.ai 3%. ChatGPT trend: 18% (2023), 23% (2024), 34% (2025), 44% (2026), with a wording change in 2026. — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/); [Pew 2025 short read](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/06/25/34-of-us-adults-have-used-chatgpt-about-double-the-share-in-2023/)
- Pew, ChatGPT use under 50 vs 50+: 57% vs 28%. A Pew chart (seen only through a search summary, **[unverified detail]**) gives 61% (18 to 29), 55% (30 to 49), 37% (50 to 64), 19% (65+). — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/); [Pew chart](https://www.pewresearch.org/chart/majorities-of-adults-under-50-now-use-chatgpt/)
- Pew, what users do (share of all adults who ever do it): searching for information 42%, work tasks 38% of employed adults, fun 25%, images or video 24%, medical advice 20%, diet and fitness 20%, news 13%, emotional support 10%, companionship 4%. — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/)

Gen Z 81% adoption; Millennials have the highest daily use at 37%; parents 84% vs non-parents 56%; household income $100K+ 77% vs under $50K 56%. — [Menlo Ventures, State of Consumer AI 2026](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2026-the-state-of-consumer-ai/)

Use: ChatGPT 47%, Gemini 39%, Copilot about 19%, Perplexity, Grok and Claude about 5% each. — [Verasight, AI adoption in 2026](https://www.verasight.io/reports/ai-adoption-in-2026)

Parents underestimate this: 51% of parents say their teen uses chatbots. — [Pew, How Teens Use and View AI](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/02/24/how-teens-use-and-view-ai/)

### Inferences
- The defensible headline is "about half of adults use it, a quarter daily", not "most people use AI". Daily use near 25% in three independent US surveys is the most reliable single figure.
- Growth now comes mostly from people aged 30 to 64 and from the heavy use of existing users. Under-30s are near saturation in rich countries (64 to 81% depending on survey).
- For Korea, the 60+ gap is extreme. Media Panel use of 6.3% (60s) and 0.7% (70+) against 69.5% (20s) means an app that reaches older Koreans is reaching almost untouched ground, but also people with no habits or vocabulary for AI.
- Survey method changes headline numbers by 10 to 20 points. Probability panels (Pew) run lower than opt-in online panels (Menlo, Verasight, YouGov), partly because online panels over-represent internet-heavy people. This is an inference from method, not a measured comparison.

### Gaps
- No single survey gives a clean country table across the US, EU, UK, Japan and Korea with the same question and timing.
- Korea's age breakdown by frequency (daily vs occasional) was not found; the Media Panel gives use only.
- The Ofcom 2026 primary report and its age figures were not opened.

## 2. What do retention and churn data show for AI apps compared with other consumer apps? What separates daily users from people who try once and stop?

### Takeaway
There are two very different pictures. The big general assistants, ChatGPT above all, keep users unusually well: about half of desktop users are still active after 12 months, and older sign-up groups use it more over time. Paid AI apps from smaller makers churn faster than non-AI apps (21% vs 31% of annual subscribers kept after a year). What separates daily users is breadth (using AI for many different jobs), being shown how at work, paying, and using it for everyday life rather than only for one-off tasks. Measured "tried once and quit" data for the general public barely exists.

### Cited Findings

**Retention of the big assistants**
- a16z, State of Consumer AI 2025 (Dec 18, 2025; data from Yipit and Sensor Tower): ChatGPT's DAU/MAU is 36% vs Gemini's 21% (Yipit). Month-12 desktop user retention is 50% for ChatGPT vs 25% for Gemini (Yipit). Month-12 paid user retention is 68% vs 57% (provider not stated). Fewer than 10% of weekly ChatGPT users visited another major model provider for most of 2025. Sora's standalone app had D30 retention under 8%, while top consumer apps are above 30%. — [a16z, State of Consumer AI 2025](https://www.a16z.news/p/state-of-consumer-ai-2025)
- a16z Top 100, March 2026: about 20% of weekly ChatGPT web users also use Gemini in a given week. ChatGPT and Gemini show best-in-class paid subscriber retention in the US (Yipit; no figures given). On mobile, ChatGPT has 2.2x Gemini's sessions per user per month. — [a16z Top 100, March 2026](https://www.a16z.news/p/top-100-gen-ai-consumer-apps-march)
- **Conflict:** multi-homing was under 10% for most of 2025 but about 20% by early 2026. These come from different periods and possibly different measures. — sources above
- YipitData panel: among US panelists paying for ChatGPT in December 2025, six-month retention was 80.4% for those not also paying for Claude vs 72.5% for those who were. — [YipitData](https://ask.yipitdata.com/insights/chatgpt-claude-dual-subscriber-retention)
- Earnest Analytics, card transaction panel **[older: Feb 2024, cohorts up to Oct 2023]**: about 89% of ChatGPT paying customers remained after one quarter and 74% after three quarters; most other paid LLM tools kept 20 to 40% after three quarters. ChatGPT's curve bends upward later (a "retention smile"), meaning lapsed payers come back. — [Earnest Analytics](https://www.earnestanalytics.com/?p=18197)
- OpenAI and NBER working paper "How People Use ChatGPT" (Chatterji, Deming et al., Sept 2025; OpenAI-authored, not peer reviewed): earlier sign-up cohorts send more messages per weekly user than newer ones, and use rises within every cohort over time. Non-work messages rose from about 53% (June 2024) to 73% (June 2025), driven mainly by changing behaviour within cohorts. Messages are 49% asking, 40% doing and 11% expressing. — [NBER w34255](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34255)

**Retention of AI apps in general vs non-AI apps**
- RevenueCat, State of Subscription Apps 2026 (published March 2026, reported by TechCrunch; subscription apps using RevenueCat, mostly smaller makers, not ChatGPT itself): annual subscriber retention after 12 months is 21.1% for AI apps vs 30.7% for non-AI apps; monthly-plan retention is 6.1% vs 9.5%; weekly-plan retention is 2.5% vs 1.7% (one secondary source says 1.2%). At the median, AI apps' annual subscriptions are cancelled 30% faster. AI apps convert trials to paid better (8.5% vs 5.6%) and earn more per user (monthly realised lifetime value $18.92 vs $13.59), and refund more (median 4.2% vs 3.5%). TechCrunch suggests users hop between apps to chase the newest technology. Reported sample sizes differ between write-ups (75,000+ developers and $11B, vs 115,000+ apps and $16B). — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/10/ai-powered-apps-struggle-with-long-term-retention-new-report-shows); [RevenueCat report page](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps)
- Cross-category mobile app baseline: about 25% of users remain on day 1, 11 to 13% on day 7 and 5 to 7% on day 30; one blog attributes about 7% on day 30 to Adjust's 2026 benchmarks. **[unverified: secondary compilations only, the page was unavailable when I tried to check it]** — [Panto](https://www.getpanto.ai/blog/mobile-app-usage-statistics)

**Ever used vs still using (measured lapse)**
- Denmark, 11 AI-exposed occupations (about 18,000 responses linked to official records, fielded Nov 2023 to Jan 2024; **[older]**): more than half had ever used ChatGPT and 41% had used it for work, but only 34% had used it in the past two weeks (29% for work); 7% had an active Plus subscription. — [Humlum & Vestergaard, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11725873/)
- US Census Bureau, Household Trends and Outlook Pulse Survey (March 2026; sample size not given in the article): 56% of workers used AI on the job for at least one of 11 tasks. Of these, 24% used it every day of the previous week, 46% on some days and 31% not at all that week. — [US Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/08/ai-use-at-work.html)
- KISDI STAT Report 26-05 (Korea Media Panel, 2024 to 2025; published around May 14, 2026): respondents split into new users in 2025 (16.8%), sustained users (7.7%) and potential users or non-users (72.9%). Over 78% of potential users had never heard of generative AI or only heard the name, with almost no change in a year (effect size d=0.03). Sustained users have moved beyond work and study into hobbies (11.4%) and everyday conversation (4.4%). Paid use rose from 14.0% to 19.5%. The three groups sum to 97.4%; the report does not explain the remaining 2.6%. — [KISDI, STAT Report 26-05 notice](https://www.kisdi.re.kr/bbs/view.do?key=m2101113055776&bbsSn=114983)
- Verasight: 15% of AI users use it less than once a month; 21% of past-month users say AI replaced a task they used to do themselves. — [Verasight](https://www.verasight.io/reports/ai-adoption-in-2026)
- Malwarebytes survey: 43% say they have stopped using ChatGPT and 42% Gemini. Sample size, population and dates are not stated, and the respondents are likely security-minded readers, so this cannot be generalised. **[vendor survey, method unknown]** — [Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/computing/people-are-quitting-chatgpt-and-gemini-over-privacy-concerns-survey/)
- ChatGPT mobile uninstalls jumped more than 295% in March 2026 after OpenAI's agreement with the US Department of Defense (Sensor Tower data, reported second-hand). A "2.5 million quit in a day" figure appears only in opinion posts. **[secondary; the "2.5 million" figure is unverified]** — [BGR](https://www.bgr.com/2163817/why-are-people-getting-rid-of-chatgpt/)

**What separates heavy users**
- Menlo 2026: daily use is 50% among payers vs 26% among non-payers; 63% of agent users use AI daily; 24% of users spend over an hour a day with AI, 10% over two hours, 29% under 15 minutes; 52% say they use AI more than a year ago. Users average 3.0 assistants (2.2 in 2025). — [Menlo](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2026-the-state-of-consumer-ai/)
- Menlo 2026: 22% of consumers use a general AI assistant daily, more than ride-sharing (6%) and food delivery (7%) combined. — [Menlo](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2026-the-state-of-consumer-ai/)
- Gallup worker survey (Jan 26 to Mar 24, 2026; 15,482 employed adults, 7,845 AI users): regular (daily or weekly) users report faster work far more often than irregular users (79% vs 38%), and higher-quality work (59% vs 26%). — [Gallup, AI benefits at work unevenly distributed](https://news.gallup.com/poll/714602/benefits-work-unevenly-distributed.aspx)
- Gallup Q2 2026 (search summary only; not on the Gallup pages I opened): 45% of workers who use AI for one or two kinds of task report productivity gains, vs 90% of those using it for seven or more. **[unverified: seen only in a search summary]** — [Gallup indicator page](https://www.gallup.com/699797/indicator-artificial-intelligence.aspx)
- Denmark (11 occupations, Humlum & Vestergaard, NBER w33777, revised March 2026): with no employer push, about 41% of workers have used chatbots at work and 7% use them daily. Encouragement alone raises take-up to 76% and daily use to 18%. Encouragement plus an enterprise tool plus training gives 93% take-up and 28% daily use, and 19% of those workers save more than an hour a day. — [NBER w33777](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777)
- Reuters 2026: 32% of daily AI users have used an agent for a real task vs 6% of those who use AI less than weekly. Main reasons for using AI: faster, easier answers (64%), talking in plain language (56%), pulling together a lot of information (54%); only 21% say it is more reliable than other sources. — [Reuters Institute](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/generative-ai-and-news-report-2026-standalone-tools-information-infrastructure)
- Pew: confidence using chatbots ("extremely or very confident") is 31% at 18 to 29, 23% at 30 to 49, 12% at 50 to 64 and 6% at 65+. — [Pew, by age](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/how-opinions-and-use-of-ai-differ-by-age/)
- Academic: a Frontiers in Psychology study (Zhao & Liu, Aug 2026) finds that worry driven by privacy and misinformation risk raises intention to stop using generative AI, and that dependence on AI weakens this effect. It measures stated intention only, not actual quitting. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13501034/)

### Inferences
- "AI apps churn" and "ChatGPT retains" are both true. A general assistant that covers many jobs retains like a utility; a single-purpose AI app (one trick, one subscription) churns faster than ordinary apps. For a local assistant, this argues for many small everyday jobs rather than one feature.
- The strongest measured driver of daily use is someone showing people what it is for (employer encouragement nearly doubles take-up in Denmark; manager support doubles frequent use in Gallup's data, see section 7). Being given access alone helps much less (enterprise tools alone: 57% vs 41%).
- In Korea, the gap is not lapsing; it is never starting. Sustained users are 7.7% and the unexplained remainder (possibly lapsed users) at most 2.6%, while 72.9% have not started and mostly do not know what it is.
- ChatGPT's within-cohort shift toward non-work use (53% to 73%) suggests people stay when it becomes part of daily life (advice, planning, conversation), not only work tasks. This matches KISDI's finding that sustained Korean users have moved to hobbies and everyday conversation.

### Gaps
- No public, method-transparent D1/D7/D30 retention for free users of ChatGPT, Gemini or Korean apps.
- No large study follows people who tried a chatbot once and asks why they stopped. Pew, Eurostat and Verasight ask never-users or current non-users, which mixes never-started with stopped.
- Gallup's breadth figure (45% vs 90%) could not be confirmed on the page I opened.

## 3. What reasons do non-users and quitters give?

### Takeaway
"I don't need it / I'm not interested" is the top reason in almost every survey (48 to 65%). Privacy, distrust of accuracy and "don't know how" come next, but their size depends heavily on how the question is asked: privacy ranges from 7% (Eurostat, likely one main reason) to 76% (Menlo, agree-style statements). Cost and fear of being judged are rarely reasons (3% and 3%). Korea is the exception: its main user panel finds "don't know how or hard to learn" is the top reason (63.5%), though the government's larger survey finds "no interest or need" on top (49.5%). Most US non-users say they will not start within a year.

### Inferences
- Single-choice "main reason" formats (Eurostat) put almost everything into "no need". Rating each reason separately (Pew, Menlo) makes privacy and distrust look like majority concerns. Privacy is a widely held background worry but rarely the deciding reason on its own.
- "No need" mostly means "I can't picture what I would use it for", not "I tested it and it was useless". Evidence: KISDI's finding that most Korean non-users barely know what it is; Persol's Japanese workplace finding that people can't imagine which tasks it fits (from a search summary only, **[unverified]**: [Persol](https://note.com/persolrandc/n/n68734ea3ea37?hl=en)); and Verasight's "haven't found a use" wording.
- For older people and Koreans, "don't know how" weighs more. For young non-users, it is a deliberate choice (Korean teens 68.2% "no interest or need"; Gallup's ethics objection 43%). These are two different audiences.
- Cost is almost never the barrier (3%), so "free and local" is not a selling point to non-users by itself.

### Gaps
- No survey reports reasons given by people who used AI and then stopped, separately from never-users.
- Questionnaires for the Korean user panel and MSIT survey were not available, so the Korean conflict cannot be explained.
- Crosstabs (for example, how many Pew non-users cite both privacy and no interest) were not available.

## 4. How many people know about local or offline AI, or run models on their own computer? Why is awareness so low?

### Takeaway
I found no general-population survey anywhere that asks whether people have heard of local AI, Ollama or LM Studio. Local AI does not appear in Pew's, Menlo's or Verasight's lists of tools. The best usage proxy is Ollama's own claim of 8.9 million monthly active developers (July 2026), roughly 1% of ChatGPT's weekly users, and it counts developers, not ordinary people. The likely reasons for low awareness are measurable in part (no consumer marketing, hardware cost rising in 2026, built-in cloud AI everywhere) and in part only inferred (jargon, no felt need).

### Cited Findings

**Usage figures and their limits**

Ollama also sells a paid cloud service, so not every Ollama user runs models locally. — [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/ollama-65m-series-b-theory-ventures-open-models); [Las Vegas Sun (Business Wire release)](https://lasvegassun.com/news/2026/jul/09/ollama-raises-65m-series-b-funding-to-grow-its-ope/); [Startup Fortune](https://startupfortune.com/ollama-raises-65-million-as-it-grows-to-nearly-9-million-developers/)

**Awareness**
- The general-population surveys I read either name no local tool (Pew's list: ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Meta AI, Grok, Claude, Character.ai) or report nothing below 40% awareness (Verasight). Menlo's 2026 report does not mention local, on-device, offline or open-source models at all. — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/); [Verasight](https://www.verasight.io/reports/ai-adoption-in-2026); [Menlo](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2026-the-state-of-consumer-ai/)
- Trust follows familiarity: across 9 countries, 37% trust ChatGPT, 27% Gemini, 18% Copilot and 13% Claude. — [Reuters Institute](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/generative-ai-and-news-report-2026-standalone-tools-information-infrastructure)

**Reasons that can be partly measured**
- **Defaults and built-in AI.** In a single week, 57% of people in 9 countries saw AI answers in search engines, 25% on their phone, tablet or computer, and 21% in social or messaging apps; only 20% saw no AI anywhere ([Reuters](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/generative-ai-and-news-report-2026-standalone-tools-information-infrastructure)). - **Hardware and price (2026).** DRAM contract prices rose as much as 98% in Q1 2026, with another 58 to 63% forecast for Q2 (TrendForce, **[secondary]**) ([AI Weekly](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/trendforce-dram-prices-to-jump-63-again-in-q2)). Gartner projected "AI PCs" at 31% of the PC market by end of 2025 and 55% in 2026, though an "AI PC" label does not mean enough memory to run a useful chatbot ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-08-28-gartner-says-artificial-intelligence-pcs-will-represent-31-percent-of-worldwide-pc-market-by-the-end-of-2025)).

### Inferences
- Local AI is a developer category, not a consumer one. Ollama's 8.9 million developers is about 1% of ChatGPT's weekly users, and the general public has had no reason, channel or word for it.
- Awareness is low for structural reasons: (1) no consumer marketing; Ollama runs on about 14 staff while Google, Meta and Kakao push cloud AI into products people already open every day; (2) "local", "open-weight", "LLM", "quantised" and "VRAM" mean nothing to ordinary users (inference; no survey measures this); (3) memory prices rising sharply in 2026 make "your own computer can do this" harder to promise, especially on cheap laptops; (4) the two classic benefits, privacy and price, are weak motivators. Privacy is cited more than it is acted on (section 5), and cloud AI is already free.
- An app for non-technical people will probably do better if it never says "local model" up front and instead names a job ("keeps your files on your computer", "works without internet"), introducing local AI as the way it works rather than the selling point. This is an inference from the job-framing findings in sections 6 and 7.

### Gaps
- No consumer survey (any country, including Korea) measures awareness of local, offline or open-source AI, or of Ollama and LM Studio. This is the biggest gap for the project.
- No independent active-user count for Ollama, LM Studio, Jan or GPT4All. All figures are company-reported or vendor estimates.
- No data on what share of household PCs could run a useful model (for example, RAM or graphics memory distributions for ordinary, non-gaming PCs).

## 5. Do people truly care about privacy and owning their data, or is that stated more than acted on?

### Takeaway
Concern is widespread and growing, but it mostly stops people at the door rather than changing how users behave once inside. Users share health, emotional and work-confidential information at scale, and privacy-first products stay tiny (DuckDuckGo about 0.7% of search worldwide). The exception is when protecting privacy costs one tap at the moment of choice: when Apple asked, about 65% of iPhone users refused app tracking. So privacy is a real factor but only shapes behaviour when it is free, easy and offered at the right moment.

### Cited Findings

**What people say**
- Pew (Feb 2026): 71% of US adults say AI will make personal information less secure (3% more secure). 54% of non-users name privacy as a major reason for not using chatbots. 59% have little or no confidence in companies to develop and use AI responsibly. — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/); [Pew, why non-users](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/why-dont-people-use-chatbots/)
- Deloitte Connected Consumer 2025 (about 3,500 US consumers, Q2 or June 2025; reported sample sizes vary between 3,524 and 3,800): 70% are concerned about data privacy and security with generative AI (60% in 2024); only 27% have high trust that tech providers keep their data secure; 24% report a data privacy issue while using generative AI. **[secondary]** — [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/409465/deloitte-gai-use-calls-attention-to-data-privacy.html); [Deloitte survey page](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/telecommunications/connectivity-mobile-trends-survey.html)
- Menlo: 76% of holdouts cite privacy concerns. — [Menlo](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2026-the-state-of-consumer-ai/)
- University of Michigan National Poll on Healthy Aging (n=2,883 aged 50+, Feb 2025): 92% want to know whether information comes from a person or AI; 46% have little or no trust in AI health information. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12762744/)

**What people do**
- Chatbot use rose from 33% to 49% of US adults (2024 to 2026) in the same period that 71% said AI makes their information less secure. — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/)
- Sensitive uses (share of all US adults who have ever done it): medical advice 20%, emotional support 10%, companionship 4% (Pew). 23% have used AI for mental-health or emotional support, including 34% of 18 to 29s (Verasight). — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/); [Verasight](https://www.verasight.io/reports/ai-adoption-in-2026)
- AARP: 41% of adults over 50 who use AI have asked an AI tool a health question (June 2026), while 69% of older adults (users and non-users) say they are not comfortable sharing personal health information with a health AI tool. These are two different questions, but they point to the same gap between what people say and do. — [AARP, AI health tools](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/artificial-intelligence-health-tools/)
- Menlo: consumers have given agents access to email (36%), web browsers (33%), messaging (31%), cloud storage (29%), calendars (27%), health apps (23%) and financial accounts (20%). The base is not stated (probably agent users). 32% of AI users have let AI act for them without a final sign-off at least once. — [Menlo](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2026-the-state-of-consumer-ai/)
- Cisco Consumer Privacy Survey 2024 **[older]**: 30% of regular generative AI users say they enter personal or confidential information, including financial and health details, even though 84% are concerned about that data going public. — [ChannelDrive](https://channeldrive.in/market-research/strong-relationship-between-privacy-awareness-and-trust-in-ai-cisco/)
- Workplace telemetry, Harmonic Security (security vendor; over 3 million prompts and uploads across 300 AI tools, US and UK organisations): about 22% of files and 4.37% of prompts sent to AI tools contained sensitive data (reported as Q2 2025; coverage gives conflicting periods). 26.3% of sensitive data entering ChatGPT went through personal, non-enterprise accounts. By November 2025, 26% of uploads contained sensitive data. An earlier study found 8.5% of prompts did (published Jan 2025). — [Business Wire, July 2025](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250731456105/en); [SC Media](https://www.scworld.com/news/almost-a-quarter-of-the-files-workers-upload-to-ai-has-sensitive-info); [Business Wire, Nov 2025](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251113987633/en/Over-a-Quarter-26-of-Uploads-to-GenAI-Tools-Contain-Sensitive-Data-an-Increase-of-Over-4-in-Just-Three-Months); [Campus Technology](https://campustechnology.com/articles/2025/01/22/study-1-in-10-ai-prompts-could-expose-sensitive-data.aspx)
- Privacy products stay niche: DuckDuckGo had 0.65% to 0.74% of worldwide search in mid-2026 and 1.53% in the US (June 2026), vs Google at about 86.7% in the US. — [Statcounter, worldwide](https://gs.statcounter.com/search-engine-market-share); [Statcounter, US](https://gs.statcounter.com/search-engine-market-share/all/united-states-of-america/)
- Counter-evidence (people act when it is one tap): Apple's App Tracking Transparency prompt has an industry-wide opt-in rate of about 35% among users shown it (Adjust, Q2 2025), meaning about 65% refuse tracking. AppsFlyer reported about 50% using a different denominator. — [Adjust](https://www.adjust.com/blog/att-opt-in-rates-2025); [Advanced Television (AppsFlyer)](https://www.advanced-television.com/2025/04/24/data-50-of-app-users-now-consent-to-tracking/); [Flurry, method](https://flurry.com/blog/att-opt-in-rate-monthly-updates)
- Counter-evidence (people act on values events): ChatGPT uninstalls rose more than 295% in March 2026 after the Defense Department deal. **[secondary]** — [BGR](https://www.bgr.com/2163817/why-are-people-getting-rid-of-chatgpt/)
- Older adults: data privacy is the top barrier to adopting new technology among adults 50+, and trust, privacy and data security are the top concerns for about half of those who hesitate (AARP, n=3,838, Sept 9 to Oct 6, 2025). — [AARP 2026 tech trends](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/2026-technology-trends-older-adults/)
- Academic evidence is mixed: a study of 709 Gen Z consumers (Frontiers in Psychology, April 2026) finds the privacy paradox in an AI try-on context; a 2026 study of Israeli students reports no paradox in its sample (abstract only seen). **[thin evidence]** — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13106007/); [IJIKM Vol 23](http://iisit.org/Vol23/IISITv23Art03Weinberger14199.pdf)

### Inferences
- Stated privacy concern does two things: it keeps some non-users out (54% "major reason" in Pew), and it influences which tool a user picks (36% of users name security and privacy). It rarely stops a current user from sharing sensitive things, because the benefit is immediate and the risk is invisible.
- The Apple tracking prompt shows the condition under which people act: a clear, one-tap choice at the moment it matters, with no loss of function. For a local assistant, privacy should work the same way: built in and noticeable at sensitive moments (health, money, work files) rather than a reason people must first understand and value.
- The most promising audience for "your data stays here" is probably the privacy-worried non-user and the sensitive-use moment (health questions among people 50+, work files people aren't supposed to upload), not the average user. This is untested.

### Gaps
- No evidence was found that consumers pay more, or switch tools, for AI that keeps data on their device.
- No crosstab shows how many privacy-worried non-users would use AI if it were local.
- No Korean data on privacy behaviour with chatbots was found. Korea's privacy regulator suspended new DeepSeek downloads in early 2025 (general knowledge, not checked in this session).

## 6. How do ordinary people discover AI tools, and what actually drives word of mouth?

### Takeaway
I found no survey that asks people how they first heard of an AI tool (friends, work, TikTok, school). The measurable channels are: (1) AI built into things people already use (search, phone, messaging), which reaches more people than deliberate use but turns into chatbot use slowly; (2) work, where encouragement nearly doubles adoption; (3) visual, shareable moments such as the Ghibli images and Nano Banana, which added tens of millions of users in days; and (4) family, since a third of AI payers have a relative or friend paying. In Korea, putting ChatGPT inside KakaoTalk reached 13 million cumulative users in about nine months.

### Cited Findings

**Built-in exposure**
- Reuters 2026, past week, 9 countries: 57% saw AI answers in search, 25% saw AI on devices, 21% saw it on social or messaging apps; 51% deliberately use AI tools weekly. — [Reuters Institute](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/generative-ai-and-news-report-2026-standalone-tools-information-infrastructure)
- Pew: 60% of US adults say they read AI summaries in search results. — [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/)
- Meta AI shows how built-in reach differs from deliberate use: Meta claims more than a billion monthly users across its apps ([SQ Magazine](https://sqmagazine.co.uk/meta-ai-statistics.md), **[secondary]**), but its standalone app had 26.6M monthly and 4.2M daily users in mid-December 2025 (Sensor Tower via [a16z](https://www.a16z.news/p/state-of-consumer-ai-2025)), and only 14% of US adults say they have used Meta AI vs 44% for ChatGPT ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/)).
- Gemini's share of AI users rose from 36% to 58% in a year (Menlo), which Menlo links qualitatively to distribution through Google Search, Android and Workspace. — [Menlo](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2026-the-state-of-consumer-ai/)

### Inferences
- The two biggest growth jumps on record (Ghibli, Nano Banana) were visual results people could see and share without learning to prompt. Word of mouth seems to spread through results ("look what it made"), not through explanations of what AI is. This is inferred from two cases, not from a survey.
- Showing people works better than telling them: encouragement and training move workplace adoption by 35 to 50 points, and KISDI recommends visible benefits for Korea's "wall of awareness".
- Built-in AI creates exposure but not a habit. Meta's billion monthly users turned into only 14% of US adults saying they use Meta AI. A separate app needs a clear repeated job to survive next to built-in tools.
- Family is an under-measured channel: a third of payers rely on a family member or friend paying, and parents are the heaviest users. For older adults, a child or grandchild setting it up is plausible but not measured.

### Gaps
- No survey of first-discovery channels (friends, work, TikTok, YouTube, school, phone defaults), in any country. This is a major gap.
- No data on referral or invitation rates for any AI app.
- No measurement of TikTok or YouTube's role in AI discovery, nor of how older adults learn from family.

## 7. What are the biggest barriers for older adults, small business owners, students and office workers?

### Takeaway
- **Older adults** lack interest in "AI" in general but are interested in specific jobs (translation 74%). They lack confidence and are worried about privacy. In Korea they are almost absent (60s 6.3%, 70+ 0.7%).
- **Small business owners** mostly say AI "doesn't apply" to them. Those who use it struggle with accuracy and fitting it to their business. In Korea, most are at a beginner level and want cost support.
- **Students** already use it (about 95%). Their barriers are unclear rules, fear of being accused of cheating, and little guidance.
- **Office workers'** use depends on the workplace: manager support, fit with existing systems and clear rules. The holdouts give habit, privacy and ethics as reasons.

### Inferences
- **Older adults:** framing by job beats framing by technology. AARP's 74% interest in AI translation against 21% interest in "AI platforms" suggests an older person should be offered "read this letter to me" or "translate this", not "an AI assistant". Voice matters too (25% of AI users 65+ use it as a main mode). In Korea the starting point is near zero, so setup has to be handled for them or by a family member.
- **Small business owners:** "doesn't apply" is the same can't-picture-the-use problem as consumers' "no need". Ready-made, trade-specific helpers (replying to reviews, writing menus or notices, sorting receipts) speak to the 54% of planners who can't find tools that fit. Accuracy (46%) is the problem for those already using it, so showing sources matters.
- **Students** don't need persuading to start. They need clear, safe-feeling ways to use it that won't be read as cheating, and their non-users object on purpose. They are probably a low-priority audience for an app aimed at non-technical people.
- **Office workers** follow their workplace. An individual app helps most where employers neither encourage nor provide AI (Denmark's 41% bottom-up baseline). Privacy and compliance (43% of holdouts) is where "nothing leaves your computer" could matter, though company IT rules may block installs. That last point is inferred, not measured.

### Gaps
- No Korean survey of older adults' reasons beyond "don't know how" (for example, trust, privacy or interest by job).
- No survey of Korean small-business owners' AI use specifically (as opposed to digital tools generally) with a known sample.
- The HEPI 42% cheating-fear figure could not be confirmed on HEPI's own page.
- No data on whether workplaces allow staff to install local AI apps.
